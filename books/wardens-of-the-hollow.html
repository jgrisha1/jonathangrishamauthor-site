About the Book

Vance Calder had a clean pitch: low-impact luxury resort, premium silence, the kind of land that had not been ruined yet. He came to the Kentucky hollow with survey flags, resort renderings, and two men who trusted his read of a situation. The road looked fixable. The locals looked like obstacles.

At the fork, a woman told them about red seed. In the field, a man named Amos Riddle told them again, this time with instructions: strip before you go back inside, scrub down, use lye soap. Do it before it sets. Vance, Miller, and Hayes heard the words. They sprayed their ankles with repellent, walked into the high grass, and kept their timeline.

The first itch arrives that night. By morning, skin is moving in ways skin should not move. When the storm takes the only road out, what remains is a barn table, boiling water, livestock medicine, duct tape, and the two people who tried to warn them. The hollow does not send rescue. Amos Riddle is not surprised.

This is not a story about punishment. The hollow is not angry. It is not watching. It is a system that has operated the same way for a long time, and three men from outside walked into it with confidence and a spray bottle. They were warned twice. The hollow does not punish. It simply continues being what it is, and the body keeps accurate records.
