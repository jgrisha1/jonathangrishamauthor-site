Queer Horror Fiction

Horror has always known what to do with queerness. The problem is that for most of the genre's history, what it did was use queerness as a marker of monstrousness. The vampire's desire is predatory and contagious. The slasher's motivation is a sexuality that cannot be named directly. The supernatural creature moves through respectable society in disguise, passing, until it does not. The coded queer villain is not an accident of the form. It is one of the form's primary moves.

What changed, slowly and then faster, is queer writers taking the genre back and using those same conventions for different ends. Horror is a form built around bodies under threat, systems that harm, the normal that turns out to be monstrous, and survival against something that wants you dead. For queer people, that is not metaphor. It is description. The genre's vocabulary fits the experience without modification.

Why Horror Works for Queer Experience

Mainstream literary fiction tends to treat queer experience as subject matter for character study: the texture of alienation, the coming-out arc, the negotiation with family and community. That is real and it matters. But horror does something different. It externalizes the threat. It gives it weight and consequence. It takes seriously the fact that the danger is not primarily internal. The closet is not a psychological metaphor in horror. It is the place where the thing lives that will kill you if you open the door wrong.

Queer horror that is doing serious work does not reduce queerness to victimhood. The queer character in contemporary queer horror is not simply the person who dies to establish the villain's danger. They are the one with knowledge the other characters do not have: knowledge of how systems operate, of what surveillance feels like from the inside, of how to move through spaces that are designed to exclude you. That knowledge is frequently what survival depends on.

Religious Trauma as a Queer Horror Vector

For queer people raised in conservative religious environments, the church functions as the horror system. This is not metaphor either. The church is the institution with authority over your body, your relationships, your understanding of your own desires. It has a theology of what you are supposed to be and a set of consequences for failing to be it. Those consequences are not abstract. They are expulsion, family rupture, community loss, and in some contexts, the genuine threat of harm.

I was expelled from IFB Bible college when my sexuality was discovered. Growing Up Independent Baptist is the account of what it was like to be raised in that system and what happened when I could not hide inside it anymore. The IFB movement is the specific system. The theological logic that makes it dangerous to queer people inside it is not unique to IFB. It runs through a wide range of conservative Christian traditions. What IFB adds is the removal of outside accountability, which removes the only check on how far the enforcement goes.

What I Write and Where It Comes From

My queer horror is not urban, not set in a city's queer community, not organized around a coming-out narrative or a story of acceptance found. It is set in East Tennessee and in the kinds of institutions that shape life in that landscape. The queer experience in my work is the experience of a body that has been told what it is supposed to be, inside systems, religious and geographical and familial, that enforce that definition with real consequences.

Angels is occult horror with queer subtext running through its premise: a man wants to see one of the beings the church told him to trust, and what he finds out about those beings is the same thing queer people find out about institutions that claim to hold their best interest. Kept Warm for the Mountain is Appalachian body horror where the queerness is structural: a body in a landscape that has its own claims on it, in a structure that has been waiting. The queer horror in my work is not announced. It is in the conditions.

I am a lay leader in the United Methodist Church and an active member of New Life UMC in Knoxville. My queerness and my faith coexist. I left the IFB. I did not leave Christianity. The horror in my religious fiction is not directed at faith as such. It is directed at systems that use the language of faith to protect authority at the expense of the people inside them. That is a specific target. It is not a general one.
