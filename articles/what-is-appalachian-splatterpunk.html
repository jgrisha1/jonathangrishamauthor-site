What Is Appalachian Splatterpunk?

Splatterpunk is the branch of extreme horror that refuses to look away. Mainstream horror implies violence, suggests consequence, lets the camera cut before the worst of it. Splatterpunk does not. It shows what happens to the body in detail, and it holds that detail long enough to mean something. The term came out of the 1980s American horror scene, associated with writers like Jack Ketchum, Richard Laymon, Edward Lee, and Clive Barker at his most visceral. What unites them is not gratuitousness for its own sake but a belief that the body is the text. If you want to write honestly about what people do to each other, you cannot soft-pedal it.

Appalachian horror is something different, though the two forms share a sensibility. Horror rooted in Appalachia is not about the hillbilly stereotype. Anyone writing that is writing about somewhere they have never been. The real thing is about specific conditions: geographic isolation that limits options and concentrates pressure, resource scarcity that makes family systems load-bearing in ways they are not in places with more slack, landscapes that are genuinely beautiful and genuinely indifferent to what happens inside the houses that sit in them, and a culture of self-sufficiency that can become, under the right stress, a culture of silence. You handle it yourself. You do not involve outside authorities. The mountain takes care of it.

Why They Go Together

Appalachian horror and splatterpunk work together because the conditions of Appalachian life make visceral extreme horror feel earned. The violence is not imported. It grows from what is already there: the isolation that removes escape routes, the family-as-institution that can become family-as-trap, the land itself pressing in on the structures people build against it. When something goes wrong in an Appalachian horror setting, it goes wrong in a way that the landscape enables and the community cannot absorb. The body pays for it.

Appalachian splatterpunk is horror that takes that setting seriously and then follows through on what it implies. It is not atmospheric in the way folk horror is atmospheric. Folk horror is interested in ritual and tradition and the community as threat. Appalachian splatterpunk is more interested in what the individual body undergoes when the systems around it fail or turn. It is present-tense. It is not elegant. Southern Gothic is literary and concerned with historical decay. Appalachian splatterpunk is concerned with what is happening right now inside the walls.

Where I Write From

I grew up in East Tennessee. The landscape in my fiction is not backdrop. It is source material. The mountains are real mountains. The houses I write about are built the way houses in that part of the country are built: close to the land, isolated from neighbors, reliant on what the family can provide internally. When I write about a structure that knows the person inside it, I am writing from a specific understanding of what it feels like to be held by a place that is not going to let you leave without a cost.

Kept Warm for the Mountain is the most direct expression of this in my catalog. The house has been waiting. It knows you are there before you know what it is. That is an Appalachian body horror premise: the land and the structure have absorbed something, and the person who arrives is going to find out what it is through their body before they understand it with their mind. SPUR sits on the border between Southern Gothic and Appalachian splatterpunk: a man comes home, something is wrong with his brother, and what is wrong turns out to be the whole system. Wardens of the Hollow puts outsiders into a Kentucky hollow and lets the land do what it does to people who do not understand what they have walked into.

What It Is Not

Appalachian splatterpunk is not poverty tourism. It is not a form of horror that uses regional identity as atmosphere for stories that could be set anywhere. The setting is not interchangeable. The specific conditions of Appalachian life, the ones I named above, are the conditions that generate the horror. Remove them and the story does not work the same way.

It is also not a subgenre of Appalachian literature in the literary fiction sense. It does not owe a debt to that tradition except in the most general way. It is horror first. The Appalachian frame is not a softening influence. If anything, it sharpens things. The land has teeth.

The Appalachian horror catalog collects the fiction from this part of my work. Each page has the full synopsis, content warnings, and severity rating. The work is not for everyone. The warnings are there so the right readers find it.
