What Is Extreme Horror Fiction?

Mainstream horror implies. It suggests violence, allows for consequence, and cuts away before the worst of it arrives. There is a camera convention in horror film where you hear what happens and see the reaction to it, and genre fiction has a prose equivalent. The damage happens between paragraphs. The reader fills it in.

Extreme horror does not do that. It stays in the room. It describes what happens to the body in detail and holds that detail until the reader has absorbed it. The term splatterpunk, which is the specific subgenre label for the literary tradition, came out of the 1980s American horror scene: Jack Ketchum, Richard Laymon, Edward Lee, Clive Barker at his most visceral. What those writers had in common was not a commitment to shock for its own sake but a belief that the cut-away is a kind of dishonesty. If you are writing about what people do to each other, the minimum honest thing is to show it.

What It Is Not

Extreme horror is not torture porn. That phrase gets used as a dismissal, and it misunderstands the form. The graphic content in good extreme horror is not decoration. It is the argument. The body is the text. What is done to the body under specific circumstances, by specific people or systems or entities, tells you something about power and about what the world actually does to people when nothing intervenes. Sanitizing that is not restraint. It is a false picture of reality.

Shock value exists as a category, and there is work that chases it without meaning. But the serious practitioners of extreme horror use graphic content the way a surgeon uses a scalpel: to get to the thing underneath. The discomfort is the point of entry, not the destination.

Who Reads It and Why

Some readers come to extreme horror because they want the reality of the form without the reassurance that mainstream horror provides. Most horror has a safety mechanism: the monster is defeated, the survivor escapes, the camera pulls back. Extreme horror does not guarantee any of that. The threat is real in the sense that its consequences are allowed to be real.

Some readers come from trauma. This is not a small number. Survivors of violence, abuse, or high-control systems sometimes find that graphic horror is more honest about consequence than literary fiction that treats suffering as background. The form does not minimize what it depicts. For readers whose experiences have been minimized, that can matter.

Some come as writers trying to understand what the form can hold. Extreme horror is technically demanding. You cannot use the cut-away, so you have to know your material at a level that most genre fiction does not require.

Content Warnings Are Not a Sign of Weakness

Every title in this catalog has content notes on its book page. This is not a concession to sensitivity. It is a practical tool. Extreme horror is not for everyone, and there is no reason for the wrong readers to have a bad experience with work that was not meant for them. The right readers know what they are signing up for. The warnings make the match easier.

Where I Write From

My extreme horror comes from specific sources. The Appalachian landscape and the isolation it creates. Religious trauma and the systems that enforce it. Queer experience of bodies that get regulated and harmed by institutions that claim authority over them. The graphic content in my work is not atmospheric. It is what those sources require when you follow them honestly.

My Baby is the harshest title in the catalog. Medical horror at maximum severity. Read the content warnings first. The Big Orange Monster Fucked Up is political splatterpunk at maximum severity. If you are new to this work, neither is the right starting point. Kept Warm for the Mountain is Appalachian body horror at severity 3 of 4. That is where most readers who want to understand what I do should start.

The full catalog has severity ratings and content notes on every book page. The rating system is not a quality ranking. It is information so you can make the right choice before you start.
